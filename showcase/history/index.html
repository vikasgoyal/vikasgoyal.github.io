    History is usually told one place at a time.

    The idea

    School teaches Rome, then China, then the Aztecs, as if they took turns. They didn't. Confucius, the Buddha and Socrates were alive within a few decades of each other. Oxford was teaching students before the Aztec capital was founded. Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid.

    Meanwhile puts eight regions of the world side by side and lets you slide through twelve thousand years, from the end of the last ice age to today, to see what was happening everywhere at once.

    How to read it

    Each plate shows the culture, state or era that best describes the region in that year, drawn as a cut-paper scene. Click a plate for its story, nearby events and the people alive there. With Real time of day on, every plate shows the real time of day in that part of the world right now.

    Drag the river, or use ← → to step, PgUp PgDn for centuries and the play button to let time run. The scale is stretched so recent centuries, which are packed with events, get more room than the long millennia of prehistory.

    About the dates

    Dates follow mainstream scholarly estimates. Anything before about 700 BCE is approximate and marked "c.", and regions rarely change on a single day, so era boundaries are a convenient simplification. There is no year zero: 1 BCE is followed by 1 CE.

    The eight regions

    Europe; the Steppe and Central Asia; East Asia; the Near East and North Africa; South Asia; North America; Mesoamerica and the Andes; and Sub-Saharan Africa. Any division of the world is imperfect. These follow how historians usually group long-running cultural regions.
